## Script

Dia sengaja menyalakan api di bawah pohon pinus, padahal tugasnya justru menjaga hutan. Soalnya, ini California tahun 1951, dan dia profesor kehutanan. Waktu itu semua orang yakin api adalah musuh nomor satu hutan. Para rimbawan negara langsung tertawa. "Awas, Si Tukang Bakar datang!" Kepala mereka sampai keluar asap dari telinga. Tapi dia cuma bilang, "Api kecil, hutan selamat." Lalu dia membakar petak hutan di Lake County, apinya cuma setinggi lutut. Tapi kampusnya sendiri melarang dia bakar-bakar di hutan riset mereka. Terus nggak ada satu pun pengelola hutan negara yang mau kasih izin. Jadi dia pindah ke hutan lain, persis di sebelah pohon sequoia raksasa. Tunggu, sequoia? Pohon setinggi delapan puluh meter itu? Soalnya, hutan yang nggak pernah terbakar itu kayak kamar yang nggak pernah disapu. Ranting dan daun kering numpuk puluhan tahun. Sekali ada percikan, apinya naik ke pucuk dan melahap semuanya. Api kecil menyapu sampah itu duluan, sementara kulit sequoia yang tebalnya bisa enam puluh senti nggak mempan. Panasnya malah membuka kerucut sequoia dan menebar ribuan biji baru. ## Fact notes

- The hero is Harold Biswell (1905–1992), a UC Berkeley forestry professor. Per the format he isn't named on screen.
- In 1951 he started understory burning in ponderosa pine at Hoberg's Resort in Lake County. That land was private, because no state or federal forest manager would let him burn.
- Critics, including federal and state foresters, called him "Harry the Torch", "Burn-Em-Up Biswell" and "Doctor Burnwell". "Si Tukang Bakar" is the Indonesian version of that nickname.
- Berkeley's School of Forestry faculty voted to deny him the school's Blodgett Forest for fire research.
- He later burned at Whitaker's Forest, a university forest next to Kings Canyon National Park among the giant sequoias. That's why the script says "hutan lain", not "hutan pribadi".
- In 1968 Sequoia & Kings Canyon National Parks started a prescribed burning program, because fire suppression had built up fuel and blocked sequoia reproduction.
- The "60 cm" bark comes from a secondary source that says "up to 2 feet thick". Double-check it against an NPS or botanical reference before publishing.
- Giant sequoias grow to about 80–95 m (General Sherman is about 84 m). Heat helps the cones open and drop their seeds.
